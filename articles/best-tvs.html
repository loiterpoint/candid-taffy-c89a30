How We Evaluate TVs

Let's be upfront: Loiter Point does not run a test lab. We don't own a colorimeter, we haven't stacked units on a bench, and you will never see us claim "we measured 2,500 nits." What we do is read the labs that do measure — carefully, and against each other.

Every pick below is synthesized from published independent testing and verified owner reports. That means RTINGS for repeatable bench numbers on brightness, contrast, input lag and color, cross-checked against TechRadar and Tom's Guide for real-viewing impressions, What Hi-Fi? for picture-and-sound judgment, and AVS Forum for the owner community's long-term reports on things spec sheets hide — panel lottery, firmware quirks, blooming in real content. When those sources disagree, we tell you. When a number comes from someone else's lab, we label it "reported" or "est. real-world," never "measured." The ranking is by evidence and merit, full stop.

Nerd-box: OLED vs. QD-OLED vs. Mini-LED — and why peak nits matter

OLED & QD-OLED are emissive. Every pixel makes its own light and can switch fully off, which is why you get perfect, per-pixel blacks and zero blooming — a bright star sits on true black with no halo. QD-OLED (Samsung's panel) swaps the color filter for a quantum-dot layer, buying more brightness and richer, wider-angle color than traditional W-OLED, at a higher price.

Mini-LED is transmissive with local dimming. Thousands of tiny LEDs behind an LCD are grouped into dimming zones — the TCL QM7K, for instance, runs roughly 1,008 zones on the 65". More zones and better processing mean tighter control, but because a zone is larger than a pixel, a bright object on a dark field can leak light into its neighborhood. That's blooming, and it's the honest trade you accept for Mini-LED's big advantage: raw brightness for the money.

Why peak nits matter. HDR content is mastered to brightness targets (often 1,000–4,000 nits); a panel that can hit higher peaks renders specular highlights — sun glints, explosions, chrome — with more punch and preserves detail instead of clipping. Peak brightness also decides how a TV survives daylight. A ~1,100-nit OLED is stunning in a dim room but can look washed out by a sunny window, which is exactly where a 2,500-nit QD-OLED or a brute-force Mini-LED pulls ahead. Match the panel to your room, not just to the spec you've heard is "best."

Bottom Line

For most people, the LG C5 OLED is the one to buy: the best all-round picture, full gaming spec, and a price that keeps drifting into the $1,100–1,500 range. If your room is bright or you simply want the best and don't care what it costs, the Samsung S95F QD-OLED is the brightest, most glare-proof panel we can point to in independent testing.

Watching your budget? The Hisense U8QG and TCL QM7K trade OLED's perfect blacks for enormous Mini-LED brightness at around $999 — cross-shop them on the day and buy whichever is cheaper in stock. And if you just need a very good TV for under $600, the Hisense U6 does the fundamentals right. There is no wrong answer on this list — only the right match for your room and your wallet.
