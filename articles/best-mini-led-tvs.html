Best Mini-LED TVs (2026)

Updated July 2026 · 5 picks ranked by the evidence

Mini-LED is where the interesting money in TVs sits in 2026: it pushes brightness that OLED still cannot match, without OLED's price ceiling or its lingering burn-in question. But the category is noisy — "up to 5,000 nits" marketing, dimming-zone counts that read like phone-camera megapixels, and real prices that swing hundreds of dollars month to month. We rank these on published independent lab testing and verified owner reports, not on price and not on what pays us. Loiter Point does not run a lab; every real-world figure below is attributed to the outlet that measured it.

How We Evaluate Mini-LED TVs

We do not have a test bench, a colorimeter, or a dark room, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. What we do is read the outlets that do own that equipment — RTINGS, Tom's Guide, TechRadar, What Hi-Fi — line up their measured numbers, and weigh them against verified owner reports for the failure modes that only show up over months (panel uniformity, firmware bugs, backlight consistency across screen sizes).

Every performance figure in the cards below is labeled by where it came from. A row marked (lab-tested) traces to a named outlet's measurement. A row marked (reported) is a manufacturer or retailer spec we could not independently confirm against a lab number. When two outlets disagree, or when a spec only holds on one screen size, we say so in the prose rather than picking the flattering figure. We rank on picture quality, brightness, processing, and value — in that order — never on the size of the commission.

One recurring caveat you'll see repeated: mini-LED performance often changes with screen size. Larger panels usually carry more local-dimming zones, so a 65-inch review does not automatically describe the 55-inch you might buy. We flag that per pick where the testing outlets flagged it.

#1

Sony Bravia 9 (K-XR90 series)

Best overall · best premium

Street ~$1,700–$3,400 depending on size · varies widely by size and sale (recently seen; verify at checkout)

RTINGS names the Sony Bravia 9 the best mini-LED TV they have tested, praising its local dimming and dark-room performance — the closest a backlit set gets to OLED-style contrast while still delivering the brightness OLED can't. It's the reference the rest of this list is measured against. What Hi-Fi rates it five stars as their best high-end mini-LED, calling the brightness and highlights "dazzling" and the built-in speaker array (roughly 70W, with height channels) a genuine cut above the norm.

Here's the candor, because What Hi-Fi was candid too: they flagged a local-dimming bug and some picture-mode inconsistency that made the set "just a little too inconsistent to be considered great" out of the box, with the caveat that Sony has been addressing these over firmware. So: the ceiling is the highest here, the floor occasionally wobbles. If you buy one, keep it updated. Gamers should also note the reviewed unit exposes only two HDMI 2.1 (4K/120Hz) ports, one of which doubles as eARC.

#2

Hisense U8QG

Best value · brightness leader

55-inch MSRP $1,499 (seen ~$999) · 65-inch MSRP $1,999 (seen ~$1,399) · recently seen at Tom's Guide review time; verify at checkout

If you want the most measured brightness per dollar, this is it. Tom's Guide put the 65-inch U8QG at roughly 3,900–4,000 nits peak HDR (10% window) and 2,908 nits in SDR — figures that leave same-price rivals like the Samsung QN80F (992 nits HDR), TCL QM7K (1,734 nits) and Sony Bravia 7 (1,507 nits) well behind. It also covers about 84% of Rec.2020 versus the roughly 80% those competitors manage, and posts an out-of-box Filmmaker-mode Delta-E of ~2.4, which is below the visible-error threshold.

The trade-offs are real and Tom's Guide listed them plainly: noticeable blooming, poor glare mitigation in bright daylight, and only three HDMI ports (all HDMI 2.1, one replaced by a USB-C video input). Critically, the ~4,000-nit number is a 65-inch result — the reviewer expects the 55-inch to top out near half that, because smaller panels carry fewer dimming zones. Buy the size you'll watch and set expectations to that size, not the headline.

#3

TCL QM8K (C8K)

Best flagship brightness · anti-blooming

Street ~$1,300+ at 65-inch on sale (recently seen; scales up sharply with size) · verify at checkout

TechRadar calls the QM8K "TCL's best mini-LED TV yet" and measured 4,240 nits — the highest number on this list, edging out even the Hisense. TCL pairs that output with its Halo Control system (a 23-bit backlight controller and dynamic light algorithm) specifically to fight the light bleed that usually rides along with brightness that high, and the reviewers credited it with solid contrast and detailed shadows. The reviewed unit carried over 5,000 local-dimming zones, among the most in the category.

Two caveats worth stating. First, TCL's marketing "up to 5,000 nits" figure relies on a brief boost mode — the sustained, measured number is the 4,240 above, which is still enormous but not the box figure (see the nerd box below). Second, gaming tops out at 4K/144Hz over two HDMI 2.1 ports, not the 165Hz you may see in spec sheets for other sets. Note also that the newer QM8L variant reportedly ships with a different (lower, ~4,000+) zone count, so match the exact model to the review you're reading.

#4

Samsung QN90F (Neo QLED)

Best for bright rooms · best for gaming

65-inch ~$1,500 (recently seen; MSRP higher) · verify at checkout

The QN90F wins a different fight. It is not the brightness champion — TechRadar measured roughly 1,915 nits peak HDR (10% window) and about 597 nits full-screen in Filmmaker mode, which trails the Hisense and TCL on raw output. What it has instead is Samsung's matte Glare-Free anti-reflection layer, which TechRadar described as making a big difference: bright windows and lamps that turn a glossy panel into a mirror mostly stop being a problem here. In a sunlit living room, a reflection-free 1,900-nit picture can beat a 4,000-nit picture you're squinting past your own reflection to see.

It's also the gaming pick. What Hi-Fi names the QN90F their best mini-LED TV for gaming, citing four full-bandwidth HDMI 2.1 ports supporting 4K/165Hz with VRR and ALLM, roughly 9.8ms input lag, and native cloud-gaming apps (Xbox, GeForce Now, Amazon Luna). The knocks: only 720 dimming zones on the reviewed 65-inch (fewer than the TCL and Sony), no HDR10+ support, and built-in sound that distorts and lacks bass. Pair it with a soundbar.

#5

TCL QM7K

Best under ~$1,000 · value sweet spot

Street ~$800–$1,000 at 65-inch (recently seen, roughly $200 under the U8QG) · verify at checkout

The QM7K is the pick when you want most of a flagship's picture and none of its price. In Tom's Guide's comparison testing it measured 1,991 nits SDR and 1,734 nits HDR (10% window) with a class-leading Filmmaker Delta-E of 1.4 — not Hisense-bright, but bright enough for most rooms and more color-accurate out of the box than the U8QG in that test. It undercuts the U8QG by roughly $200, which is why it keeps landing on best-value and best-under-$1,000 lists.

What you give up versus the Hisense, per that same Tom's Guide comparison, is brightness, some HDR color volume, and a slightly higher input lag (13.1ms vs 9.7ms). If those matter to you, pay up for the U8QG; if you mostly watch in a normal-lit room and want the lowest sane price on a real mini-LED, the QM7K is the sensible answer. It's a natural cross-shop against sets in our best TVs under $1,000 roundup.

// Nerd box: why "up to 5,000 nits" is not the brightness you'll see

Marketing nits are measured on a tiny white patch — usually a 10% window (10% of the screen lit) — often in a boost mode that only holds for a few seconds. Fill more of the screen and the number collapses. TechRadar measured the Samsung QN90F at ~1,915 nits on a 10% window but only ~597 nits full-screen in Filmmaker mode: same TV, roughly a third the brightness, depending purely on how much of the panel is lit. TCL's "up to 5,000 nits" QM8K measured 4,240 nits sustained in TechRadar's testing — the box figure leans on a brief boost the panel can't hold.

Practical translation: a bright HDR highlight (a sun, a headlight) can hit the big number; a full white hockey rink or snow field will land far lower. When you compare TVs, compare the same measurement type from the same outlet — a 10%-window figure against another 10%-window figure — and ignore the boxes.

// Nerd box: mini-LED vs OLED, and what dimming zones buy you

Mini-LED and OLED fail in opposite directions. Mini-LED lights the picture with thousands of tiny LEDs behind the LCD, grouped into local-dimming zones. More zones means finer control over which parts of the screen are dark — but because a zone still lights a cluster of pixels, a bright object on a black background can leak a faint halo into the dark around it. That's blooming, and it's the artifact every reviewer here weighs. OLED lights each pixel individually, so it has no blooming and perfect blacks, but it can't get as bright and carries a small, well-documented burn-in risk over years of static logos. What Hi-Fi's own summary: mini-LED wins on brightness and bright-room use; they still prefer OLED overall for contrast and viewing angles "at least for now."

Zone counts are a rough proxy, not a score. The reviewed QN90F (~720 zones) can look better managed than a set with thousands of poorly-controlled zones, because processing — how intelligently the TV drives those zones — matters as much as the count. It's why the Sony Bravia 9 tops RTINGS' list on local-dimming quality, not on having the biggest number. If you want the no-blooming route entirely, start with our OLED guide; for the broader field, see our QLED roundup and the all-around best TVs list.

Bottom Line

If money is no object and you'll keep the firmware current, the Sony Bravia 9 is the reference — RTINGS' best-tested mini-LED, with the best local dimming and the fewest compromises when it's behaving. For most people the smarter buy is the Hisense U8QG: the measured brightness leader per dollar, as long as you accept blooming and buy a size big enough to carry the zone count. Chasing the single highest number, the TCL QM8K measured 4,240 nits with real anti-blooming hardware.

Two situational picks close it out. If your room has windows, the matte Samsung QN90F — with 4K/165Hz across four HDMI 2.1 ports — beats brighter rivals you'd be fighting glare on, and it's our gaming lean. On a tighter budget, the TCL QM7K gets you a real mini-LED under $1,000. Whichever you pick, the built-in speakers are the weak link on every set here — budget for one of our recommended soundbars, and if you're weighing the whole field, our general best TVs guide zooms out past mini-LED alone.
